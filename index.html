I am a political sociologist mainly concerned with studying power and social networks.

Research

My work contributes to research on money in politics and congressional elections, including scholarship on campaign finance, donor networks, and the electoral advantages that shape who wins office in the U.S.A. More broadly, I’m interested in how power is organized, concentrated, and reproduced through networks.

Methodologically, my work engages social network analysis, causal inference, and computational approaches to finding, exploring, and representing the abundance of data available to modern sociologists. However, I also value a historical element, and try to keep my work centered on practical and timely questions.

Dissertation

Corporate Elites and U.S. House Elections

My dissertation explores whether and how America’s most connected business leaders help candidates win seats in Congress.

Studies of money in politics usually treat donors as cash cows. I argue that the social networks corporate donors are embedded in may be equally valuable resources. Their ties across the corporate world let them vouch for candidates, rally other donors, and open doors that money alone cannot. I test whether this social capital helps candidates win beyond the dollar sum these donors give.

In doing so, the project offers power structure research a direct test of whether elite networks translate into electoral power, and shows scholars of money in politics that who gives can matter as much as how much.
