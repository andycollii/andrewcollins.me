I am a political sociologist mainly concerned with studying power and social networks. Research

My current research is at the intersection of political sociology and social network analysis. I use computational methods to trace the ties among corporate directors, the firms they govern, and the political campaigns they fund. This informs questions of community structure and political outcomes, especially regarding elections.

Much of my work engages a long-running debate in the study of power. In particular, I have contributed to debates about the extent to which the corporate community in the United States may be characterized as politically unified or fragmented. I have also published on how the use of artificial intelligence affects public trust in American institutions, and on the international NGO networks that promote Western democracy.

Dissertation

My dissertation explores whether and how America’s most connected business leaders help candidates win seats in Congress. Studies of money in politics usually treat donors as cash cows. I argue that the social networks corporate donors are embedded in may be equally valuable resources. Their ties across the corporate world let them vouch for candidates, rally other donors, and open doors that money alone cannot. I test whether this social capital helps candidates win beyond the dollar sum these donors give. In doing so, the project offers power structure research a direct test of whether elite networks translate into electoral power, and shows scholars of money in politics that who gives can matter as much as how much.

Publications

  1. 2026

    Collins, Andrew. “Fractured or Reconfigured? A Review of the Debate on U.S. Corporate Elite Unity.” Sociology Compass 20(3):e70181. doi:10.1111/soc4.70181 PDF

    A critique of the fractured-elite thesis, with three empirical claims in support of the corporate-dominance alternative.

    Reply: Mark S. Mizruchi, “Fractured? Yes. Reconfigured? Maybe. Unified? Unlikely.” Sociology Compass. doi:10.1111/soc4.70237

  2. 2023

    Collins, Andrew, and Jason Jeffrey Jones. “Effect of Artificial Intelligence on Social Trust in American Institutions.” Journal of Social Computing 4(3):221–231. doi:10.23919/JSC.2023.0022 PDF

    A survey experiment on a sample designed to mirror the U.S. population. Trust in police, companies, and hospitals fell when they were described as using AI. Government agencies were the exception and gained slightly.

  3. 2023

    Kim, Jessica, and Andrew Collins. “Patterns of Global Democracy Promotion: Centrality in DINGO Networks, 1981–2015.” Socius 9. doi:10.1177/23780231231221462 PDF

    Uses network centrality to trace democracy-promoting international NGO networks from 1981 to 2015. Countries became more connected overall, but ties in the civil liberties network remain unevenly distributed.
