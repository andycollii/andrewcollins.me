Research

My work contributes to research on money in politics and congressional elections, including scholarship on campaign finance, donor networks, and the electoral advantages that shape who wins office in the United States.

It also engages a long-running debate in the study of power: I have contributed to debates about the extent to which the corporate community in the United States may be characterized as politically unified or fragmented, and I argue that campaign contribution networks offer one of the clearest ways to answer that question. I have also published on how the use of artificial intelligence affects public trust in American institutions, and on the international NGO networks that serve to promote Western democratic values.

Methodologically, I am interested in exploring how graph theory can be used to further our understanding of sociological problems, and much of my work circles back to this question. I tend to take a data-driven approach but also value a historical element, as I am sensitive to the limitations of statistical inference.

    Effect of Artificial Intelligence on Social Trust in American Institutions Collins, Andrew, and Jason Jeffrey Jones. Journal of Social Computing 4(3):221–231.

    A survey experiment on a sample designed to mirror the U.S. population. Trust in police, companies, and hospitals fell when they were described as using AI. Government agencies were the exception and gained slightly.

    Patterns of Global Democracy Promotion: Centrality in DINGO Networks, 1981–2015 Kim, Jessica, and Andrew Collins. Socius 9.

    Uses network centrality to trace democracy-promoting international NGO networks from 1981 to 2015. Countries became more connected overall, but ties in the civil liberties network remain unevenly distributed.
